It took a while for anthropologists to realise this. At the turn of the 20th century, scientists thought that big brains made hominids unique. This was a reasonable conclusion since the only known hominid fossils were of brainy species — Neanderthals and Homo erectus. In more recent decades, anthropologists have determined that bipedalism has very ancient roots. The earliest hominid with the most extensive evidence for bipedalism is the 4.4-million-year-old Ardipithecus ramidus. Although the earliest hominids were capable of upright walking, they retained primitive features — such as long, curved fingers and toes as well as longer arms and shorter legs — that indicate they spent time in trees. It's not until the emergence of H. erectus 1.89 million years ago that hominids grew tall, evolved long legs and became completely terrestrial creatures.
